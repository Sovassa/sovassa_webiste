Ask ten agencies what a business website costs and you will get ten very different numbers. Most of that spread is not overcharging or undercharging. The quotes describe different websites: one is a few templated pages, another is a lead-generation system with custom design, written content, integrations, and a team that can edit it after launch.

This guide explains what sets the cost, the common types of business website, the costs that sit outside the build, and how to compare quotes fairly.

The short answer

A website’s cost follows the number of decisions it has to carry, not the number of pages alone. How many offers it explains, how custom the design is, who writes the content, which systems the forms must reach, and how your team will edit it afterwards all move the price. A small site on a proven template with your own content is the cheapest honest version. A custom-designed site with researched copy, integrations, and a structure built for search sits at the other end.

What actually drives the cost

1. The job the site has to do

A site that confirms you exist is a smaller job than one that has to explain several services, qualify leads, and send each enquiry to the right person. Before asking for quotes, write down what a visitor should be able to decide or do after visiting. That sentence shapes everything else. Our guide on how to brief a website project walks through it.

2. Structure and number of page types

Page count matters less than the number of distinct page types. Ten service pages built from one well-designed template cost far less than ten pages that each need their own layout. A clear website architecture keeps the number of templates low and makes the site easier to grow.

3. Design: template, adapted, or custom

A ready-made theme is quick and inexpensive, but it shapes your content to fit it. An adapted theme adds your brand and adjusts key layouts. A custom design starts from your content and your buyers, and produces a design system your team can reuse. Custom design costs more up front and usually pays back when the site has to sell something complex.

4. Content and copywriting

Content is the most underestimated part of a website budget. If your team writes the copy, the cost moves to your calendar instead of the invoice, and launches often slip while pages wait for text. Professional copywriting, photography, and video add cost but remove the most common cause of delay. Either way, someone has to own it.

5. The content management system

WordPress, Webflow, Shopify, headless CMSs, and fully custom builds all have different costs to build, host, and maintain. The right choice depends on who will publish after launch and what the site must connect to. Our CMS comparison covers the trade-offs.

6. Integrations and functionality

A contact form that sends an email is simple. A form that creates a lead in your CRM, books a call, takes a payment, or checks stock in an inventory system is real development. Multilingual sites, member areas, calculators, and search across large catalogues all add to the scope.

7. Search, speed, and accessibility

Technical SEO, fast page templates, and accessible markup cost little when they are built in from the start and a lot when they are retrofitted. Redirects from an old site, structured data, and a sitemap belong in the original scope. So does making sure the site can be used with a keyboard and a screen reader.

Common types of business website

Type What it usually includes
Brochure site A handful of pages on a template: home, about, services, contact. Your own content, a simple form, basic search settings.
Lead-generation site Dedicated pages for each service or audience, adapted or custom design, written content, CRM-connected forms, analytics, and technical SEO.
Ecommerce site Product catalogue, cart and checkout, payment gateway, shipping and tax rules, order emails, and inventory or ERP integration.
Portal or web app Logged-in areas for customers, partners, or staff, with roles, dashboards, and data from your business systems.

Each step down the table adds design, development, and testing. Many businesses start with a strong lead-generation site and add a portal or shop later, once they know what customers need.

Costs outside the build

  • Domain and hosting. A domain renews every year. Hosting ranges from shared plans to managed platforms, and the right level depends on traffic and how critical uptime is.
  • Licences. Premium themes, plugins, fonts, stock images, and SaaS tools often carry annual fees.
  • Maintenance and security. CMS and plugin updates, backups, uptime monitoring, and security patches need an owner. An unmaintained site becomes slower and less secure over time.
  • Content updates. New pages, case studies, and articles keep a site useful and help it rank. Budget time or money for them.
  • Marketing. A website does not bring its own visitors. Search, paid campaigns, email, and social work send people to it. See why traffic does not equal leads for the other half of the problem.

How to keep the cost under control

  • Prioritise pages by value. Launch with the pages that sell and support sales. Archive pages and nice-to-haves can follow.
  • Reuse templates. A small set of well-designed page types covers most needs and keeps future pages cheap.
  • Have content ready. Late content is the most common reason a website project overruns.
  • Choose a CMS your team can use. If every change needs a developer, the site will cost more every month it exists.
  • Keep one decision-maker. Feedback by committee adds rounds of changes and weeks to the schedule.

How to compare website quotes

Quotes are only comparable when they describe the same scope. Ask each company to state clearly:

  1. Which pages and page templates are included.
  2. Whether design is template-based, adapted, or custom.
  3. Who writes and loads the content.
  4. Which integrations are included, and which are not.
  5. What SEO work is part of the build, including redirects from your current site.
  6. Who owns the site, the hosting account, and the domain afterwards.
  7. What support is included after launch, and for how long.

Frequently asked questions

Is a template website good enough for a business?

It can be, when the business is simple and the template fits your content. It becomes a problem when the template forces you to cut or bend the explanation buyers need. Start from the content, then decide whether a template can carry it.

Why do agencies charge so differently for the same website?

Usually because they are not quoting the same website. Differences in design approach, content, integrations, SEO, and post-launch support explain most of the gap. Ask for a written scope and compare line by line.

How much should I budget each year after launch?

At minimum, plan for domain, hosting, licences, updates, and backups. If the site is part of how you win business, also plan for new content and ongoing improvements based on what visitors do.

Do I need a custom website?

Not always. Custom design earns its cost when your offer is complex, when you compete on trust and expertise, or when a template would limit how you explain what you do. For a simple local service, an adapted template may be the better use of budget.

Can I build the site myself and save money?

Website builders make it possible, and for a very small business it can be a sensible start. The trade-off is your time, and the risk of missing structure, speed, and search basics that are cheaper to get right the first time.
